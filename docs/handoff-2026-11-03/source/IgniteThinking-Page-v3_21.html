How every puzzle works

A six-part design thinking loop

The puzzles change; the loop does not, so your child never has to work out how the app behaves.

  1. 01UnderstandRead the situation and say what is actually being asked.
  2. 02DefineName the real goal before choosing a method. The wrong question can still get a confident answer.
  3. 03IdeatePut down more than one way it could be done. One idea is not a choice.
  4. 04PrototypeBuild the attempt, with a picture or a tool to think with.
  5. 05TestCheck it. An attempt that fails is not the end; it is information your child reads.

An attempt that does not work comes back to Ideate.

ReflectAlways available

Reflection closes every puzzle, solved or not. A child who stays stuck is never handed the answer to move on.

And when it does not work

The loop sends your child back to choose again, with help in small steps, each one written in advance. The guide is never given the answer.

Why now

Why reasoning, and why now?

Your child can already get an answer in seconds, and increasingly, explanations too. What no tool will hand them is the experience of working something out when it is genuinely hard.

Why grades 4 and 5

From“It worked three times, so it must always work.”

To“Let me try to break it. If I can’t, here’s why.”

Grades 4 and 5 sit inside the years when math standards expect this change: from trusting a few examples to making a claim, hunting for a counterexample, and explaining why it holds. NCTM 2000

Why reasoning

“Glue”

The National Research Council’s word for reasoning: the part that turns facts, procedures and concepts into a coherent whole rather than a list of isolated rules. NRC 2001

The habits the puzzles ask for

Make sense of it, and stick with it
Look for structure
Notice what repeats
Make a case for the answer

From the Common Core Standards for Mathematical Practice: how mathematicians think, not what they know.

Reading level

Written for a fourth-grade reader in Warm-Up and a fifth-grade reader above it, so the difficulty is in the thinking, not the reading.

Kinds of thinking

Logic, patterns, planning, debugging, constraints and optimization. No coding needed.

Math puzzles too

Math puzzles on grade 4 and 5 ideas such as fractions, common multiples, proportional reasoning and volume, and math along the way in the others.

For parents · the grown-up review

You get the evidence, not a verdict.

Your own read-only view of how your child worked, in their own words and drawings. Enough to ask a better question at dinner, not enough to supervise from another room.

  • TriedEvery idea they came up with, the plan they picked, and the sketch they made.
  • HelpHow much help they opened, and at which step.
  • TestedWhat happened when they tested it, including the tries that didn’t work and what they changed.
  • SkillThe thinking skill each puzzle practiced.
  • In their wordsWhat they wrote at the end, word for word, and one question to ask them about it.
  • Over timeHow much help they needed, puzzle by puzzle, beside the ones they did on their own. No score. publish only once TC-8 Growing view ships

What it will not tell you

Help is not failure. One solve is not mastery. A longer reflection is not better thinking. A trend in how much help they needed is one line about one app, never an assessment of your child.

Access for every child

Reading and typing should not be the barrier.

A child who can think through a hard problem may still struggle to read it or type the answer. Those are separate abilities, and the app treats them that way.

Listen to any of it

Instructions, hints and worked examples can be read aloud, at the press of a button.

Speak instead of typing

On devices that can turn speech into text by themselves, your child can press and talk instead of typing.

as deployed this is Safari only (workstreams list A13, 2 Nov): no button on Chromebooks or in Chrome. Name the devices or keep as is

The sound stays on the device

Speech becomes text on your child’s device. The audio is never sent to us or kept: no storage, no training, no voiceprint.

What a family gets

  • 24 puzzles, 18 computational thinking and 6 math, across three levels, all open from the first day.
  • The guide, with every hint written in advance. Its AI, which fits the help to your child’s work, comes in a later update.
  • The journal and the grown-up review.
  • A direct line to the people who built it, and a say in what changes.

Starting without AI has its own benefits: your child sees help exactly as we wrote it, no AI responds to anything they write or say, and when the AI arrives we can tell whether it helps or gets in the way, because we will know how children did without it.
